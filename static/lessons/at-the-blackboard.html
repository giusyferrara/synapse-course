At the Blackboard

Five things explained out loud to someone who has never met an if, the way you'd explain them standing at a board with chalk in your hand. Press play on any of them. The sentence being spoken lights up — which is also where the subtitle comes from, for free.

What makes it sound like a person

  • Contractions, always. "It's", "let's", "isn't". The single strongest tell of speech.
  • Everyday thing first, code second. Never the other way round.
  • Say each thing once. Explain the mechanism on the first row, then never again.
  • Say the hard part is hard. "This line looks scary. It isn't."
  • Never read the code back. Say what the line means, not how it's typed.
  • Do it with real numbers. Five minus two lots of two is one.

This browser has no speech voices installed, so the play buttons are off. The scripts still read fine — and in production the audio comes from ElevenLabs anyway, not the browser.

And the hourglass, which already talks

The sixth one is built. It's the 6×6 hourglass in Java, four phases and four different programs that make the same picture — and its narration is where every rule above came from.

It lives in synapse-lavori/inside-the-loop.html, so its script isn't repeated here. Same voice, same two tracks: a short ticker on every step, and the teacher who only speaks when something actually changes.

What these are for

These are scripts, not videos. The dissertation feedback asked for video; recording video means re-recording it every time a lesson changes, and an on-screen avatar buys nothing a voice doesn't.

A script costs nothing to change, generates its own subtitle track (it's already text), translates, and — read once by ElevenLabs — sounds the same for thirty students or three thousand, at no cost per listen. The player is already built around "tell me when you've finished speaking", so swapping the browser voice for real audio is a one-line change.
